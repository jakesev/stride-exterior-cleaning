A grey, mouldy deck looks like a job for a pressure washer. Often it is the wrong tool. Timber is made of fibres of different hardness, and water under high pressure strips the softer ones away. The result can be a surface that looks clean when wet but dries rough, fuzzy and blotchy.

## Why timber goes grey in the first place

Sunlight is the main cause. Ultraviolet light breaks down lignin, the natural "glue" that binds wood fibres together near the surface. Rain then washes the degraded material away, leaving loose, pale cellulose fibres that weather to the familiar silver-grey.

That weathered layer is shallow, but it matters. It is loosely bound, it holds dirt and mould, and finishes don't bond well to it. Most deck-cleaning products are designed to remove it.

Finishes slow the process down. WoodSolutions, the Australian timber industry's information service, notes that finishes don't make timber waterproof, but they slow the rate of wetting and drying, which reduces cracking and splitting. The Queensland Government's timber guide for weather-exposed decking says service life is improved by applying a protective finish to all joints and exposed surfaces at construction and as regular maintenance. It also says pale-coloured finishes are preferred.

## What "furring" is and why pressure causes it

Wood isn't uniform. Each growth ring has denser and softer bands. High-pressure water erodes the softer wood between the harder grain faster, which can leave a ridged, corduroy-like surface with loose fibres standing up. People in the trade call this "furring", "fuzzing" or "raised grain".

Sources agree on the symptoms, if not every detail of the cause:

- A Canadian finishing guide warns that pressure washing can leave the wood surface "furry" and poorly suited to holding a finish. It recommends scrubbing away loose fibres before coating.
- Stain maker Olympic warns that holding a sprayer too close can permanently imprint lines and marks in the timber.
- HGTV's deck guide notes that if washing raises soft wood fibres, you'll need to sand before staining.
- Log-home finish maker Perma-Chink says pressure washing has a place for stripping old finish from bare wood, but is not recommended for routine maintenance cleaning of an existing finish.

Some of the raised texture comes from wood simply swelling as it absorbs water, and light sanding usually fixes it. Gouges and "wand marks" from a close, narrow jet are harder to remove and may need heavier sanding.

None of this means water pressure must never touch timber. Some timber-cleaner labels call for a strong rinse, and Intergrain's instructions for stripping a coating in poor condition combine its cleaner with power washing. The issue is pressure that is too high, a jet that is too narrow, or a nozzle held too close.

## A gentler way to clean a deck

The Queensland Government's deck maintenance advice starts with less water, not more: avoid frequently wetting decks and balconies, and sweep instead of hosing where possible. When a proper clean is due, a gentler sequence works well:

1. **Sweep thoroughly**, including between boards, so leaf litter isn't left to hold moisture.
2. **Apply a timber or deck cleaner** made for the job. For grey, weathered timber, oxalic-acid-based cleaners such as Intergrain UltraPrep Timber Cleaner and Feast Watson Woodclean are designed to remove degraded fibres and brighten the wood. Follow the label for dilution and dwell time. Intergrain's sheet gives 10 to 15 minutes without letting it dry.
3. **Scrub along the grain** with a stiff-bristle brush.
4. **Rinse** as the label directs, keeping any pressure low with a wide fan and at a sensible distance. Work with the grain, not across it.
5. **Let it dry** before coating. Intergrain says to allow at least an hour after cleaning before applying its decking oil. Cooler, cloudier days are better for coating. Cabot's says not to apply its decking oil in direct sunlight in hot weather.

Intergrain notes that severely weathered or degraded timber should be sanded back to a fresh surface before cleaning, and that a light sand between coats removes any raised grain.

### New hardwood and tannin bleed

New hardwood decks often bleed tannin, a water-soluble brown-red stain, onto nearby concrete, pavers and render. Timber supplier Bowens notes that merbau and blackbutt are more prone to this than ironbark or spotted gum. Bleeding can continue for months on high-tannin species, and cleaning usually reduces tannin marks rather than eliminating them. If you have a new deck above a light-coloured slab, it's worth planning for this before the first wet season.

## Fences

Fences raise the same issues as decks, with a few differences:

- **Both faces matter.** The shaded side of a fence, often the neighbour's, usually grows more mould. Agree on access before cleaning.
- **Garden beds sit right at the base.** Cleaning solution and rinse water drain straight into them, so wet and protect plants first.
- **New palings need time.** Bunnings' team advises letting new timber palings weather for around six to 12 weeks before oiling or staining, then coating before the surface starts to grey.
- **Test the colour.** Treated pine can take oils very differently from hardwood, so try a small area first.

## How often to re-oil in Queensland sun

There's no single correct interval. Manufacturers base it on condition rather than the calendar:

- **Cabot's** says to inspect coated timber every 12 months for loss of colour or water repellency, then clean with its deck cleaner and apply two coats.
- **Intergrain** says protection and ease of maintenance are best if its UltraDeck oil is recoated before the coating and timber underneath start to deteriorate. It requires a third coat in harsh conditions: full sun, around pools and in humid areas.
- **The Queensland Government** says to reapply finishes at regular intervals depending on the finish and how exposed the deck is, and to clean thoroughly before recoating.

Published rules of thumb vary. RACV's adviser suggests every six to eight months for oil-based coatings and every eight to 12 months for water-based finishes. *Complete Home* suggests nine to 12 months for oils and 12 to 18 months for stains under average conditions. Decks in full sun, near pools or on the coast need attention at the shorter end.

A simple test helps: sprinkle water on the boards. If it beads, the finish is still working. If it soaks straight in and darkens the timber, it's time to clean and recoat.

## Look underneath while you're there

Cleaning is a good time to check the structure. The Queensland Government recommends inspecting decks at least once a year for decaying timber, unstable balustrades and corroded fixings. It also suggests removing shrubs or plants that permanently shade a deck, which helps it dry and slows mould growth.
