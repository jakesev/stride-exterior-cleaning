Outdoor paving takes a lot: sun, rain, leaf litter, foot traffic and, around a pool, a steady supply of salt and chlorinated water. Most of it cleans up well. The trick is knowing which material you have, because a method that suits concrete pavers can permanently mark natural stone.

## Know your stone

The Natural Stone Institute groups natural stone into two broad families:

- **Siliceous stone** is made mainly of silica-based minerals. Sandstone, granite and bluestone (basalt) sit broadly in this group.
- **Calcareous stone** is made mainly of calcium carbonate. Limestone, travertine and marble belong here.

The difference matters because **acids dissolve calcium carbonate**. On travertine, limestone or marble, even mild acids such as vinegar can etch the surface. That is physical damage, not a stain, and sealing doesn't prevent it.

### Sandstone

Sandstone is relatively soft and porous. Brisbane supplier Sandstone World advises against acidic cleaners and high-pressure hoses, and against abrasive and alkaline cleaners, which can scratch and dull the surface. Gosford Quarries recommends low pressure only and lists hydrochloric acid, vinegar, baking soda and bleach as substances to avoid on sandstone.

Sandstone also varies a lot. UK paving maker Marshalls notes that some sandstones don't react to acid-based cleaners while others turn orange, depending on their iron content. Always test a hidden area first.

### Travertine and limestone

These are the most acid-sensitive stones you're likely to find around a home. Use a pH-neutral cleaner made for stone, a soft brush and low-pressure water. Sandstone World also warns that a stone sold or remembered as sandstone may in fact be limestone, which is acid-sensitive. If you aren't sure, treat it as acid-sensitive.

### Bluestone and basalt

Bluestone is dense and durable, but stone-care supplier Stone Doctor notes it can still be damaged by acidic cleaners and sometimes by solvent-based sealers. A neutral stone cleaner is the safe default.

### Clay pavers

Advice on using acid on clay pavers varies between manufacturers and depends on the stain. One UK paving maker describes a diluted hydrochloric acid treatment for lime staining, but warns that the same acid turns vanadium efflorescence into a dark stain. That's a good reason not to reach for acid without identifying the stain first. Think Brick Australia's *Clay Masonry Cleaning Manual* is the Australian reference for identifying stains and choosing the right treatment.

### Concrete pavers

Concrete pavers handle a surface cleaner at an appropriate pressure. They are not acid-friendly. National Masonry's cleaning guide warns that hydrochloric acid may cause irreparable surface damage to concrete pavers.

## Efflorescence: the white bloom

A white, chalky haze on new or damp paving is usually efflorescence: soluble salts carried to the surface by water and left behind when it evaporates. BRANZ, New Zealand's building research body, notes that efflorescence doesn't affect the performance of concrete, brickwork or blockwork. It is a cosmetic problem.

The common mistake is washing it. Water re-dissolves the salt, so it seems to vanish and then returns as the surface dries. Think Brick Australia recommends brushing efflorescence off with a stiff, dry brush once the surface has dried out. Collect the dust rather than sweeping it into joints. If it persists, specialist efflorescence cleaners exist, but check that they suit your material.

## Joint sand

The sand between pavers locks them together and helps keep weeds and ants out. Water under pressure removes it easily. In fact, pressure washing is how landscapers deliberately strip out failing joint sand before re-sanding. A UK guide to maintaining sandstone paving also warns against blasting sand out of joints.

After any thorough clean, check the joints and top them up where needed. Polymeric jointing sand, which hardens once wetted, can help discourage weeds and ants. It has trade-offs: it must be applied to a completely dry surface following the manufacturer's steps, and it can leave a hard-to-remove haze on paver surfaces.

## Slip safety around pools and paths

Green and black growth on paving isn't just a looks problem. The Royal Horticultural Society notes that algae and moss make paths and steps slippery, and a study of a brick walkway in South Africa found that algae colonisation produced a surface hazardous to people.

Around pools, slip resistance is designed in. Industry summaries of Standards Australia's handbook HB 198 list a pendulum classification of P4 and a wet-barefoot classification of B as guidance for swimming pool surrounds, and P5 and C for ramps and stairs leading into water. These are design guidance, not legal requirements, and ACO's summary notes that wear and contamination also affect how slip-resistant a surface is in use. Regular cleaning keeps a surface performing as it was designed to.

Sealers can change grip. Topical, film-forming sealers tend to be slipperier than penetrating ones, which is one reason manufacturers often suggest penetrating sealers for wet outdoor areas.

## Pool chemistry and stone

### Salt

Salt water from a pool can soak into porous stone and crystallise as it dries. Stone suppliers describe the results as efflorescence (a white crust) and spalling (small pits and flakes on the surface), sometimes called "salt water attack". Simple habits help: hose down the surround after heavy splashing, and sweep off any white crust early. Gosford Quarries describes sealing sandstone as optional in salt-free settings but highly recommended where it is in contact with salt water. If you seal, use a penetrating, stone-appropriate sealer, and only on dry stone. Sandstone World advises keeping stone dry for 24 hours after sealing.

### Chlorine and acid

Liquid pool chlorine is sodium hypochlorite, the same chemical used in many exterior cleaning solutions. Mixed with acid, it releases chlorine gas, which the NSW EPA describes as highly toxic. WorkSafe Victoria has documented an incident where a pool's automatic dosing system did exactly this.

At home, this means three things:

- Never use an acid-based paver cleaner and a chlorine-based cleaner together or one straight after the other without thorough rinsing.
- Don't decant cleaning chemicals into containers that have held pool chemicals.
- Don't rely on container colours. The NSW EPA notes that pool chlorine is often in light blue containers and acid in black, but chlorine sourced from Queensland comes in black containers.

It's also sensible to keep cleaning runoff out of the pool itself, and to let whoever looks after the pool know what was used on the surround.

## A simple routine

For most paving and pool surrounds in South-East Queensland:

1. **Sweep regularly** to remove leaves and organic matter that feed growth.
2. **Rinse salt splash** off natural stone after heavy pool use.
3. **Clean with the right product.** Use pH-neutral stone cleaners on natural stone and avoid acids on calcareous stone and concrete pavers.
4. **Use low pressure on stone** and moderate pressure on sound concrete pavers.
5. **Check and top up joint sand** after a thorough clean.
6. **Reseal on condition**, not by the calendar, with a sealer suited to the stone.

How often a surround needs a professional clean varies with shade, tree cover and pool use. Stride assesses stone type before choosing a method, and that's worth asking any contractor to do.
