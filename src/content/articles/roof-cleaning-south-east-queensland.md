## What you are actually looking at

Roofs in South-East Queensland sit in a warm, humid climate with a long wet season. At Brisbane's city weather station, the Bureau of Meteorology records a mean of 176 mm of rain in February and 149 mm in March, and morning humidity that sits in the mid-60s per cent through late summer and autumn. Redlands records are wetter again, with an annual mean of about 1,270 mm. That warmth and moisture suits the organisms that colonise roofs.

What homeowners usually notice falls into three groups:

- **Dark streaks and staining.** These are generally a mix of biological growth (algae and mould), airborne dirt and, closer to the bay, salt. Dulux's roofing care guidance lists salt, dirt, mould, algae and lichen as the build-up a roof wash is meant to remove.
- **Lichen.** The flat, crusty grey, white, yellow or orange patches. According to the Australian National Botanic Gardens, the lichens found on concrete roof tiles are the same ones that grow naturally on limy soils and rock: to them, concrete is no different from limestone. Lichens grow slowly, which is why patches spread gradually over years rather than months.
- **Moss.** Soft green cushions, usually where water lingers: in shade, along tile laps, in valleys and near overhanging trees.

UK roofing manufacturer Marley notes that moss and lichen favour shady, damp conditions near trees, and that shallow-pitched roofs stay damper for longer. In Australia the shadier slopes are generally the south-facing ones, which is why growth is often heavier on one side of the house than the other.

### Is it doing harm?

Opinions differ, and the honest answer is "it depends". Some tile manufacturers note that moss and lichen do not eat into tile surfaces but may need removing because they slow run-off and send debris into gutters. Monier, a major Australian tile manufacturer, links moss and lichen growth to moisture and dirt build-up. The practical problems are real, though: heavy growth holds water against the roof, sheds material into gutters and valleys, and makes it harder to spot cracked tiles or failed pointing. Light growth on a sound roof is mostly cosmetic. Heavy growth, blocked gutters or a leak are reasons to act.

## Different roofs, different rules

There is no single "roof clean". The material decides the method.

| Roof type | What tends to grow | Main cleaning caution |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete tile | Lichen, moss, dark streaks in the tile texture | Manufacturers advise against pressure washers; older tiles and ridge pointing can be damaged |
| Terracotta tile | Lichen and moss, especially on shaded slopes | Older tiles can be brittle; walking on them risks cracking |
| COLORBOND® / Zincalume® steel | Grime and mould in pans and around fixings; salt near the coast | Rain washes most of the roof; sheltered areas need gentle washing with fresh water |
| Coated or restored roof (membrane) | Mould, algae and dirt on the coating | High pressure and harsh chemicals can damage the coating |

### Tiled roofs

Monier "strongly advises against the use of pressure washers for roof cleaning". It also recommends clearing gutters, downpipes and valleys at least once a year (more often under trees) and having a qualified roofing professional inspect the roof, ideally every five years.

Tile roofs also have ridge capping held in place with bedding and sealed with pointing. Cracked or loose pointing is a common source of leaks, and a jet of water aimed at an already-failing joint can push water where it shouldn't go. If pointing is cracked, that is a job for a roofer before or instead of cleaning.

### COLORBOND® and Zincalume® steel

BlueScope's maintenance guidance makes a useful distinction. Surfaces that are regularly washed by rain, which includes most roof sheeting, do not usually need routine washing. "Unwashed" areas, sheltered from rain, do: fascias, the underside of eave gutters, wall cladding under eaves, carports and patios. BlueScope recommends washing those areas at least every six months, or every three months in coastal locations with salt spray. Its recommended method is fresh water, with a mild, non-abrasive detergent for stubborn marks followed immediately by a thorough rinse. It also advises disconnecting rainwater tanks from the relevant gutters if detergent is used, and avoiding hard, mineral-rich water such as bore water.

### Coated and restored roofs

Many older tile and metal roofs in SEQ have been restored with a membrane coating. Dulux's AcraTex care guidance recommends washing roughly once a year (more often near the coast) using low pressure (under 450 psi), a soft broom and a low-foaming, biodegradable cleaner. It says heavy staining should never be removed with a high-pressure water blaster or harsh chemicals, and warns that some mould treatments, including bleach and sugar soap, may damage a membrane film. Dulux's care page also notes that pressure washing has the potential to damage any underlying coating.

## Why "more pressure" is the wrong instinct

High-pressure cleaning looks dramatic, and on a concrete driveway it can be appropriate. On a roof it creates two problems:

1. **Surface damage.** Coatings, glazes and the outer surface of older tiles are what protect the roof from weather. Manufacturers caution against high pressure precisely because it can strip or damage them.
2. **Water in the wrong places.** Roofs are designed to shed water running downhill, not to resist a jet aimed up under laps, flashings and ridge capping.

### The soft-wash approach

"Soft washing" is the term commonly used for the gentler alternative that manufacturers' guidance points towards: apply a suitable cleaning solution at low pressure, give it time to work on the growth, then rinse with low-pressure water. The right product and strength depend on the roof. A cleaner suitable for bare concrete tile may not suit a membrane coating, which is why Dulux specifically warns about bleach-based treatments on its coatings. Good practice also includes protecting gardens and paths from run-off and, as both BlueScope and Dulux note, disconnecting downpipes that feed rainwater tanks before any cleaning product goes on the roof, then rinsing thoroughly before reconnecting.

Some lichen does not disappear on the day. Dead growth can take a period of weather to loosen and wash away, so a fair result is judged over weeks, not minutes.

## Why this is not a weekend DIY job

The Queensland Government's ladder safety campaign reports that more than 1,000 Queenslanders are hospitalised each year after falling off ladders, and that most of these injuries happen around the home. A University of Queensland study of ladder-fall patients at Princess Alexandra and Nambour hospitals found most falls happened during home maintenance, men over 55 made up more than half of cases, and patients spent an average of five days in hospital. Half reported worse psychological wellbeing six months later.

A roof adds hazards a ladder alone does not:

- Wet roofs, especially with cleaning solution on them, are slippery.
- Tiles can crack underfoot, and Marley notes that foot traffic during moss removal can itself damage tiles.
- Getting on and off a ladder at the gutter line is a known high-risk moment.
- Many SEQ homes are two storeys or highset, increasing the fall height.

Even for trained workers, WorkSafe Queensland's position is that ladders should only be used after safer options have been considered and ruled out. The Queensland Government's own ladder advice starts with a simple question: do you need to be up there at all?

## A sensible approach for homeowners

- **Look from the ground.** Binoculars or a phone camera zoomed in from the yard will show streaking, lichen and obvious broken tiles.
- **Fix before you clean.** Cracked tiles, failed pointing, rust or flaking coatings need a licensed roofer. The QBCC recommends having your roof checked by an appropriately licensed contractor.
- **Trim overhanging branches** where safe to do so. Less shade and leaf litter means slower regrowth and cleaner gutters.
- **Ask about method before price.** Anyone cleaning your roof should be able to explain how the method suits your roof material and age, and what they will do about tanks, gardens and gutters. That is the approach Stride takes: assess the roof first, then choose a low-pressure method, and decline roofs that can't be worked safely.
