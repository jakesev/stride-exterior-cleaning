Solar panel cleaning and whether it's worth it

Solar panels in South-East Queensland get a lot of free rinsing from summer storms, so it's fair to ask whether cleaning them ever matters. The honest answer is sometimes, and how they're cleaned matters as much as whether they are.

Key takeaways
  • An even film of dust usually costs only a few per cent of output, but concentrated soiling like bird droppings can have an outsized effect.
  • Rain rinses panels well in heavy falls, but light showers, flat tilts and stuck-on deposits leave soiling behind.
  • Manufacturers warn against high-pressure water, abrasives and harsh chemicals, and some say improper cleaning can void the warranty.

Does dirt actually reduce output?

Yes, but usually less than people expect, and unevenly.

Solar panels work by letting light through the glass to the cells underneath. Anything sitting on the glass blocks some of that light. The International Energy Agency's Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme (IEA PVPS) has studied this "soiling" in detail. Its 2022 report on soiling losses was summarised in Australian coverage as finding that soiling typically reduces output by around 3 to 5 per cent globally, with much larger losses in dry, dusty regions. One of its co-authors, from the Australian PV Institute, stressed that monitoring soiling rates is the way to decide when cleaning is worthwhile.

For a typical suburban roof in a high-rainfall area like ours, that is a useful reality check. A light, even film of dust is unlikely to be costing you much.

When it matters more

The exceptions are where soiling is heavy or concentrated:

  • Bird droppings. A small experimental study published in 2024 found that when droppings covered roughly 30 per cent of a test panel's area, its output fell by about 35 per cent. A 2023 study in Case Studies in Thermal Engineering found bird droppings caused far more efficiency loss than an even layer of dust. Concentrated deposits also create "hot spots": shaded cells that heat up while the cells around them keep working. Panels have bypass diodes to limit the effect, but persistent or recurring hot spots can eventually cause permanent damage.
  • Leaves and debris lodged along the bottom edge of the frame, where water pools and grime collects.
  • Salt on bayside properties, which leaves a film that light showers don't fully shift.
  • Low-tilt or flat-mounted panels, which shed water and dirt more slowly.

Doesn't the rain clean them?

Often, yes. A 2026 paper in EPJ Photovoltaics notes that in locations with high rainfall, natural cleaning by rain may keep modules sufficiently clean. Brisbane certainly qualifies in summer: the Bureau of Meteorology's Brisbane station records a mean of roughly 138–176 mm a month from December to March.

But rain is selective:

  • It needs to be heavy enough. The IEA PVPS 2025 soiling fact sheet describes models in which rain only counts as "cleaning" above a daily threshold, such as more than 5 mm in a day, and another scenario where rain removes only some particles.
  • SEQ's dry season is the dirty season. From July to September Brisbane averages around 30 mm of rain a month and only about four rain days, so dust, pollen and smoke accumulate for months.
  • Tilt matters. Research on test modules in Arizona found soiling losses on flat (0°) panels were roughly double those on panels tilted at 23° or 33°. A University of Queensland study found the optimum fixed angle for Brisbane panels is about 26° facing true north, but many roofs here are lower-pitched than that.
  • Stuck-on deposits stay stuck. Bird droppings, lichen at the frame edge, and dried salt are not reliably moved by rain.

How to tell if your panels need cleaning

The best guide is your own data, not the look of the glass from the street.

  1. Check your monitoring app or inverter. Compare output on clear days this month with clear days at the same time last year. A gradual drop with no other explanation can point to soiling.
  2. Look for patterns, not just dirt. Bird droppings, a line of grime along the bottom edge, or a single panel that looks different are worth attention. A uniform light dust film usually isn't.
  3. Rule out other causes. New shade from a growing tree, a fault, or an inverter error can all look like a dirty-panel problem. Anything electrical is for your solar installer.

Panels that already look clean are not worth paying to clean.

What manufacturers say about cleaning methods

Panel makers publish cleaning instructions in their installation and maintenance manuals, and they are consistent on the main points:

GuidanceWhat manufacturers say
Water pressureJinko advises against jet washing and high-pressure cleaning. Trina sets a maximum water pressure in its manuals and recommends soft water.
ToolsUse soft cloths, sponges or soft-bristled brushes. Jinko warns that sharp tools can damage the glass or backsheet. Heckert Solar warns sharp items can damage the anti-reflective coating.
ChemicalsTrina advises avoiding alkaline or strong acid solvents.
Water qualityMidsummer and Heckert Solar recommend deionised or demineralised water, which dries without mineral spots.
TemperatureMidsummer recommends ambient-temperature water and cleaning early, before panels heat up, to avoid thermal shock. Trina limits the temperature difference between water and module.
WarrantyTrina states that damage caused by inappropriate cleaning will void its warranty. Jinko says damage from sharp tools may limit warranty benefits.

Your panels' manual is the final word, because instructions vary by brand and model. If you don't have it, the manufacturer's website usually lists manuals by model number.

Why deionised water?

Ordinary tap water contains dissolved minerals. When it evaporates on glass, those minerals are left behind as faint spots, which is not ideal on a surface whose whole job is letting light through. Deionised or reverse-osmosis water has had most of those minerals removed, so it dries clear without needing detergent or squeegeeing. That is why panel makers recommend it.

Why not detergent?

Manufacturers restrict which cleaning agents can be used (Trina, for example, rules out alkaline and strong acid solvents), and any residue left on the glass is something else for light to pass through. If your manual permits a specific mild cleaner, follow it exactly. Otherwise, water and a soft brush are the safe default.

Safety: the bigger reason to be careful

Cleaning panels nearly always means working on a roof, and that is where the real risk lies. The Queensland Government reports that more than 1,000 Queenslanders are hospitalised each year after falling from ladders, mostly around the home. Wet panel glass is slippery, and panels are not a safe place to put your weight.

If you want to clean panels yourself, the safest approach is from the ground: a soft brush on an extendable pole and a gentle hose rinse, early in the morning. Do not:

  • climb onto the roof, especially on wet tiles or sheeting
  • use a pressure washer, abrasive pad, scraper or household cleaning chemicals
  • spray cold water on hot panels in the middle of the day
  • touch wiring, isolators or the inverter.

Professional cleaning should follow the same rules: deionised water, a soft brush, no pressure, and nothing electrical. It's the approach Stride uses on solar arrays across the Redlands and bayside Brisbane.

The bottom line

For most SEQ homes, rain does a reasonable job for much of the year and a light film of dust costs very little. Cleaning earns its keep when panels are low-pitched, near the bay, under trees, or a favourite perch for birds, and especially after a long dry spell. When panels are cleaned, gentle is the rule: soft water, soft brush, low pressure and cool glass.
