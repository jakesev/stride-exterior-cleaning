House washing in SEQ: mould, algae and how often to clean

Warm, wet summers and shaded walls give mould, mildew and algae everything they need. Here's what is growing on your walls, how often the people who make your cladding say to wash it, and what a careful house wash looks like.

Key takeaways
  • Growth is worst on damp, shaded walls, so the shaded side of a house usually needs attention first.
  • Manufacturers' guidance for render, paint, fibre cement and Colorbond suggests regular washing, from every six months to every two years depending on the product.
  • A good house wash plans for plants, pets, water tanks and runoff before any cleaning solution goes on.

If one side of your house has gone grey-green while the rest looks fine, you're not imagining it. In South-East Queensland, the shaded side of a house, eaves that never see the sun and walls behind thick planting are where organic growth takes hold first.

Why SEQ walls go green

Organic growth on buildings needs moisture, shelter and time. The UK's Royal Horticultural Society lists damp, humid, shady conditions and poor drainage as the main causes of algae, moss and lichen on hard surfaces. The NZ Metal Roofing code of practice makes the same point about cladding: sheltered and shady environments are "particularly conducive" to lichen growth.

Brisbane's summers supply plenty of moisture. In summer 2024–25, for example, the Bureau of Meteorology recorded 561.2 mm of rain in Brisbane over summer 2024–25, 128% of the long-term summer average of 437.6 mm. Add a wall that dries slowly because it faces away from the sun, and growth follows.

Mould, mildew, algae: what's on the wall?

People use these words loosely, and for cleaning purposes the distinction matters less than you might think. As a rough visual guide:

  • Mould and mildew usually show up as grey, black or speckled patches, often on eaves, soffits and the upper parts of shaded walls.
  • Algae tends to look green and slightly slimy, especially low on walls and on surfaces that stay damp.
  • Lichen forms crusty, often round patches and holds on more tightly than the others.

All are organic growth that lives on the surface. Dulux's UK product data notes that its fungicidal wash is effective only on surface growth. Where mould has been painted over and is trapped between coats, Dulux says all existing coatings need to be removed. Washing can't reach growth that is sealed under paint.

Surface by surface

Render and texture coatings

Rockcote asks owners to clean its render systems every 12 months and to check monthly for dust, mould, dirt and cracking. Where pressure is used, it specifies about 500 psi through a fan tip, never a needlepoint jet. Dulux's Acratex texture coating guidance suggests washing every 12 to 18 months, more often near the sea. Natural lime and clay finishes are more delicate. Rockcote says not to use bleaches, mould treatments or high-pressure sprays on them.

Painted weatherboard

Paint is a protective layer, and keeping it clean helps it last. Dulux New Zealand says that hosing alone, even at good pressure, is "not usually sufficient" to remove the normal build-up of grime, and suggests washing painted walls every two years to maintain the life of the paint. Its house-wash product isn't for coatings less than 72 hours old.

Paint that is already flaking, blistering or chalking is the main risk in any wash. Water finds the edges of a failing coating. Better to know about that before cleaning starts.

Fibre cement

James Hardie's maintenance guidance recommends washing fibre cement every six to 12 months with a garden hose, working top-down in small sections, with a mild detergent and soft brush for stubborn marks. It warns that acid and high-pressure washing can damage the surface. (This is from James Hardie's North American site, so check the Australian literature for your product.) For painted fibre cement, it defers to the paint manufacturer.

Colorbond walls, fascias and garage doors

BlueScope notes that surfaces washed naturally by rain, such as most roof sheeting, don't usually need this maintenance. Sheltered areas do: wall cladding under eaves, fascias, garage doors and the underside of gutters, carports and patios. BlueScope recommends washing these at least every six months, or every three months in coastal areas with salt spray. It says to use a soft brush or sponge with mild detergent, and to avoid bore water.

How often should a house be washed?

The honest answer is that it depends on exposure. A shaded, sheltered wall near the bay needs more frequent attention than an open, sunny one. Manufacturers' guidance gives a useful starting range:

SurfaceManufacturer guidance
Colorbond (sheltered areas)At least every 6 months; every 3 months on the coast (BlueScope)
Fibre cementHose wash every 6–12 months (James Hardie, US guidance)
Rockcote renderClean every 12 months (Rockcote)
Acratex texture coatingEvery 12–18 months, more often by the sea (Dulux)
Painted wallsEvery two years (Dulux NZ)

Much of this guidance describes light maintenance washing, which owners can often do with a hose and a soft brush. A full professional clean is usually needed less often, when growth has built up beyond what a light wash will shift.

Protecting plants, pets, windows and water tanks

Soft washing usually involves a cleaning solution, often based on sodium hypochlorite. That makes preparation just as important as the cleaning.

  • Plants. Sodium hypochlorite is an oxidiser and can damage plant tissue through overspray, drift or runoff. Common practice is to wet plants thoroughly before the job, keep them wet during it and rinse them afterwards so solution doesn't dry on leaves. Cover delicate or valued plants where possible.
  • Pets and people. Australian safety data sheets for sodium hypochlorite list eye damage and skin irritation or burns as hazards. Keep pets inside or well away until the area has been rinsed and dried, and keep children's toys and pet bowls out of the work zone.
  • Windows and doors. Close them before work starts. Glass, frames and sills should be rinsed with clean water so no residue dries on them.
  • Rainwater tanks. If the wash will reach gutters that feed a tank, disconnect the downpipe first. BlueScope advises disconnecting tanks when detergent is used on roofs and gutters. Dulux gives the same advice for coated roofs and says to rinse with potable water before reconnecting.
  • Runoff. Redland City Council lists wastewater from outdoor cleaning among contaminants that must be kept out of stormwater, and Brisbane City Council investigates roof and driveway cleaning runoff. Wash water should be directed to lawn or garden rather than the street gutter where practical.
  • Never mix chemicals. Sodium hypochlorite and acid release toxic chlorine gas. Queensland Health also warns against mixing bleach and vinegar.

What to expect on the day

A careful house wash usually follows a predictable sequence:

  1. A walk-around first. The cleaner checks the wall materials and their condition, notes any flaking paint or cracked render, and agrees what's in scope.
  2. Preparation. Plants are wetted or covered, outdoor furniture moved, windows and doors closed, and tanks disconnected if needed.
  3. Application. The cleaning solution goes on at low pressure, with extra attention to eaves and shaded walls.
  4. Dwell time. The solution is left to work for a set period. This is when the cleaning happens, not the rinse.
  5. Rinse. Walls, plants and surrounding areas are rinsed at a pressure the materials can handle.
  6. A final check together. Anything that didn't come off, and why, should be explained.

You'll usually need an outside tap with steady flow, and access to every side of the house in scope. At Stride, the walk-around at the start and the end is part of the job.

Cleaning won't fix paint that has already failed, render that is already cracked, or stains that have soaked into a surface. It should leave sound surfaces clean and undamaged, and give you an honest picture of the rest.
