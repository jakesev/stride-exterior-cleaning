Pressure washing vs soft washing: which surface needs which

Pressure washing and soft washing are two different tools, not two settings on the same machine. Knowing which surfaces can take force, and which can't, does most of the work of stopping a clean from turning into a repair.

Key takeaways
  • Pressure washing removes dirt by force. Soft washing uses a cleaning solution, time to work and a low-pressure rinse.
  • Makers of render, roof coatings, fibre cement, roof tiles and Colorbond steel all advise gentle or low-pressure cleaning, and some advise against pressure washers altogether.
  • Condition matters as much as material. A sound surface and a failing one can need different treatment, so check before you clean.

Most people think of exterior cleaning as one job: point a pressure washer at the dirt until it's gone. On a sound concrete driveway that works. On a rendered wall, a painted weatherboard or a coated roof, the same approach can cause damage that no amount of cleaning will undo.

This guide explains what each method actually does, which surfaces suit each one, and what the manufacturers of common Australian building products say about cleaning their materials.

Two methods, two jobs

Pressure washing relies on force. Water leaves the nozzle at high pressure and knocks dirt and growth off the surface. On flat concrete it is often done with a rotary surface cleaner, which spreads the force evenly instead of concentrating it in a single jet.

Soft washing relies on chemistry and time. A cleaning solution is applied at low pressure, left to work (the "dwell time"), then rinsed off gently. The name exists mainly to set it apart from pressure washing.

You can see the soft-wash approach in an industry code. The New Zealand Metal Roofing Manufacturers' code of practice describes removing lichen from roof and wall cladding by applying a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution with a low-pressure spray, broom or brush, leaving it for about five minutes, then rinsing thoroughly with cold water. No high pressure is involved.

Why force is the wrong tool for some surfaces

Render and texture coatings

Rockcote's maintenance guide asks owners to clean their render system every 12 months. Where a pressure cleaner is used, it specifies about 500 psi through a fan tip rather than a direct needlepoint, which it warns "may lift the sturdiest finish". It also warns against holding the tip too close to the wall.

Not all render is the same. Rockcote's guide for its natural clay and lime finishes is stricter. It lists high-pressure sprays among the things to avoid, and it says not to use commercially available bleaches or mould treatments. Before anyone cleans a rendered wall, it is worth knowing what the render actually is.

Painted surfaces and fibre cement

James Hardie's maintenance guidance says a low-pressure water spray and a soft, non-metal brush suit fibre cement best. It warns that "acid and high pressure washing can damage the fibre cement surface". Where a pressure washer is used anyway, it recommends a wide fan tip, at least six feet (about 1.8 metres) from the wall, and under 1,500 psi. That guidance comes from James Hardie's North American site, so check the Australian product literature for your cladding. James Hardie also notes that damage caused by improper cleaning may not be covered by its warranty.

For painted finishes, James Hardie refers owners to the paint manufacturer. Paint that is already flaking or blistering is the main risk. Water under pressure finds the edge of a failing coating and lifts it.

Roof tiles and roof coatings

Roof tile maker Monier "strongly advises against the use of pressure washers for roof cleaning". It also notes that simply walking on a tiled roof risks damaging tiles, and it recommends leaving roof work to a qualified professional because of the fall risk.

Coated roofs have their own rules. Dulux's maintenance guide for its Acratex roof membranes allows a low-pressure water blast of less than 450 psi, using a fan jet of cold water at 45 degrees, kept at least 30 cm from the surface. It says heavy staining should not be removed with a high-pressure water blaster or harsh chemicals, and that bleach or sugar soap may damage the membrane film.

There is a useful lesson in the fact that Dulux's roof restoration brochure describes pressure washing at a minimum of 4,000 psi as part of preparing a roof before it is coated. Stripping a surface back to recoat it is a different job from maintaining a coating you want to keep.

Colorbond steel

BlueScope's maintenance bulletin for Colorbond steel says washing should be done with a sponge, soft cloth or soft-bristle nylon brush, with no abrasive scourers or steel wool. It says to scrub gently to avoid scuffing, using a mild solution of pure soap or non-abrasive detergent in warm water. BlueScope's home maintenance guide adds: never use solvent cleaners such as turps, petrol, kerosene or paint thinners.

Timber

High pressure can tear the softer wood between the growth rings, leaving a fuzzy or "furry" surface that usually needs sanding. Stain maker Olympic warns that holding a sprayer too close can permanently imprint lines and marks in the wood. There's more on this in our guide to timber decks and fences.

Soft natural stone

Sandstone is the obvious example. Brisbane supplier Sandstone World advises against both acidic cleaners and high-pressure hoses on sandstone, and Gosford Quarries recommends low pressure only. Our guide to pavers and natural stone covers this in detail.

Where pressure washing earns its place

Sound concrete and most concrete paving can take a surface cleaner at an appropriate pressure. Even here, pressure and technique matter:

  • Too much pressure, too close, can etch the surface of concrete and leave visible marks.
  • Exposed aggregate can lose stones if the surface paste around them is worn away.
  • Joint sand between pavers is easily blasted out. Pressure washing is how landscapers deliberately remove failing joint sand, so expect to re-sand after a clean.

Soft washing has its own rules

Gentle on the surface does not mean careless with the chemistry.

  • Sodium hypochlorite is hazardous. Australian safety data sheets classify stronger solutions as causing serious eye damage and skin burns, and as toxic to aquatic life.
  • Never mix it with acid. Chlorine and acid together release chlorine gas, which is highly toxic. This is a known hazard with pool chemicals, which use the same active ingredient.
  • Runoff is regulated. Brisbane City Council lists car, roof and driveway cleaning runoff among the water pollution it investigates. Redland City Council includes wastewater from outdoor cleaning among the contaminants that must be kept out of stormwater.
  • Bleach is not a cure-all. Queensland Health's mould guidance notes that bleach may not be effective at killing mould on porous surfaces. Some finishes, such as natural lime renders and Acratex roof membranes, shouldn't have bleach on them at all.

The rule underneath

There is no single "safe" pressure for a house. The right method depends on the material, its age and its condition, and on what its manufacturer says. BlueScope suggests trying any cleaning method on a small, inconspicuous area first. That's sensible advice for any surface.

At Stride, choosing between the two methods is treated as the core of the job rather than a detail. Whoever does the work, it's fair to ask them which method they'll use on each surface, and why.
