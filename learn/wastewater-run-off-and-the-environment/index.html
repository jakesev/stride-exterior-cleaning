Wastewater, run-off and the environment: the Queensland rules

The water that comes off a driveway or wall during cleaning doesn't just disappear. In Queensland it is a regulated contaminant, and where it ends up is the cleaner's responsibility.

6 min read14 sourcesReviewed October 2026

Key takeaways
  • Waste water from outdoor cleaning is a prescribed water contaminant in Queensland, and letting it reach a gutter or stormwater drain is an offence.
  • Council guidance generally allows water carrying only soil and organic matter onto lawn or garden; water containing chemicals or oil needs proper collection and disposal.
  • Ask any cleaner where the water will go, what products they use, and how drains, gardens and pools will be protected.

Stormwater drains are designed to carry rain away from streets and properties. In most of South East Queensland, what goes into a street gutter travels through pipes and channels to local creeks and, eventually, Moreton Bay. Redland City Council operates hundreds of stormwater quality devices to trap litter and sediment before it reaches the bay, but the system is built to carry rainwater, not to treat what is washed into it.

That is why Queensland law treats cleaning water as a pollutant in its own right.

What the law says

Cleaning water is a prescribed contaminant

The Environmental Protection Regulation 2019 lists, in Schedule 10, the substances that are "prescribed water contaminants". The list includes waste and waste water from outdoor cleaning, with examples such as high-pressure water blasting of premises, plant, roofs, streets and vehicles. It also includes chemicals, oil, paint, sediment and plant matter.

Notice what the entry covers. It is the waste water from the cleaning process itself, not only the cleaning product. Water that has picked up dirt, mould, oil residue or paint flakes from a surface is covered, whether or not a detergent was used.

Letting it reach a gutter or drain is an offence

Under section 440ZG of the Environmental Protection Act 1994, it is an offence to unlawfully deposit a prescribed water contaminant into waters, a roadside gutter or a stormwater drain, or to leave it somewhere it could reasonably wash into one. Council guidance notes that the offence applies whether the release was deliberate or not.

The offence is enforced by local councils. Redland City Council's water pollution guidance names steam and high-pressure cleaning businesses among those that must prevent prescribed water contaminants from entering the stormwater system, and lists waste water from outdoor cleaning as a form of water pollution. Council fact sheets elsewhere in Queensland describe penalties of 15 penalty units for individuals and 75 penalty units for corporations for this offence; check the current Act for up-to-date figures.

The general environmental duty

Separately, section 319 of the Act sets a general environmental duty: anyone carrying out an activity that causes, or is likely to cause, environmental harm must take all reasonably practicable measures to prevent or minimise it. Amendments that took effect in 2024 made it an offence in its own right to breach this duty where the breach causes, or is likely to cause, serious or material environmental harm.

In practical terms, a cleaning business is expected to plan for where its water goes before the work starts.

What council guidance allows

Queensland council fact sheets for mobile cleaning businesses describe a fairly consistent approach:

  • Water carrying only soil and organic matter can generally be directed onto a lawn or garden area where it can soak in, rather than flow to the street.
  • Water containing oil, chemicals or other contaminants must not go to stormwater. It needs to be collected and either taken by a regulated waste business or, with approval, discharged to sewer.
  • Discharge to sewer requires approval from the local water authority. In the Redlands, Council approval is required before trade waste is discharged to sewer, and approvals can include pre-treatment and other conditions.

Some councils also suggest holding tanks for collecting pressure-cleaning water so it can be disposed of properly.

What responsible operators do

The rules describe outcomes rather than prescribing equipment, so operators meet them in different ways. Common approaches include:

  • Planning the flow before starting. Looking at which way each surface falls, where the nearest gutter or stormwater pit is, and where water can safely soak into lawn or garden.
  • Protecting drains. Temporarily blocking or covering nearby stormwater inlets, or using barriers (often called bunding) to stop water reaching the gutter.
  • Collecting water. Using recovery equipment to collect water from the work area, for example where a driveway falls straight to the street, then disposing of it appropriately.
  • Choosing products carefully and using only what's needed. Using the least amount of product that does the job, keeping concentrated product away from drains, and being able to name the products used. A product labelled "biodegradable" can still be a contaminant: the regulation covers outdoor cleaning waste water regardless of what is in it.
  • Protecting gardens, ponds and pools. Keeping chemical-bearing water and overspray away from plants, fish ponds and pools, and rinsing as needed.
  • Containing solids. Sweeping up loosened debris, paint flakes and sediment rather than washing it into the street.

No single method suits every property. What matters is that the operator has thought about your site and can explain their approach.

What homeowners should ask

You don't need to know the legislation in detail to get a useful answer. Before booking, ask:

  1. Where will the water go? Ask them to point it out on your property.
  2. What will stop it reaching the street gutter or a drain? Listen for a specific answer, not "it'll be fine".
  3. What products will you use? Ask for product names. If you have pets, a fish pond or particular plants you care about, mention them.
  4. How will you protect my garden, pool and neighbours' property?
  5. Is run-off control included in the quote? If one quote includes it and another doesn't, they aren't describing the same job.

It is reasonable to be wary of an operator who says run-off doesn't matter, or who plans to wash everything down the driveway into the gutter.

If you clean it yourself

The same law applies to everyone. Redland City Council's guidance for residents suggests directing water from vehicle washing onto a vegetated area and keeping sediment, mulch and rubbish contained. Applied to home cleaning, that means:

  • Sweep up loose dirt and leaves before wetting a surface.
  • Use as little product as practical, and follow the label.
  • Direct water onto lawn or garden rather than the street.
  • Don't rinse paint, oil or chemical residue into a gutter or drain.

Water use and restrictions

Exterior cleaning also uses mains water. Seqwater's most recent water security update we reviewed (July 2026) did not report any restrictions, and its water security program sets out when they would apply: region-wide restrictions begin when combined dam levels fall to 50% of capacity, with tighter levels below that. If you are booking during a long dry spell, check current status with Seqwater or your water retailer (such as Redland Water or Urban Utilities).

Reporting pollution

If you see cleaning water, paint or chemicals going into a drain, Redland City Council asks residents to contact Council. Council notes that incidents in major waterways are handled by the Queensland Government's environment department. In Brisbane, contact Brisbane City Council.
