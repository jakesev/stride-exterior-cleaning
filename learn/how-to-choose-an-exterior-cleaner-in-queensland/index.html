How to choose an exterior cleaner in Queensland

Most exterior cleaning jobs go smoothly, but the ones that don't usually go wrong before work starts. A handful of plain questions about insurance, scope, run-off and safety will tell you most of what you need to know.

Key takeaways
  • Ask for a written quote that names each surface, the method and what is excluded, and keep it.
  • Ask to see a current certificate of currency for public liability insurance, and check the business name on it matches the quote.
  • Uninvited door-to-door offers come with a 10 business day cooling-off period, and the trader cannot take payment or start work during it.

Hiring someone to clean the outside of your home involves water under pressure, chemicals, ladders and your property. You don't need to be an expert to tell a careful operator from a careless one; you only need to know what to ask, and what a reasonable answer sounds like.

Start with the scope, not the price

Before comparing prices, make sure you are comparing the same job. Two quotes for "house wash and driveway" can describe very different amounts of work.

A useful quote names:

  • Each surface to be cleaned (for example, rendered walls, eaves, front path, double driveway, pool coping).
  • The method for each surface, such as high-pressure cleaning for sound concrete, or low-pressure washing with a cleaning solution for render, paint and timber.
  • What is excluded, such as second-storey windows, roof surfaces or stain types that may not fully come out.
  • How run-off will be managed (more on this below).
  • The price, whether it includes GST, and when payment is due.

Quotes and estimates are not the same thing

Consumer agencies draw a distinction between an estimate, which is a rough idea of cost, and a quote, which is generally a firm price for the work described. The Northern Territory Government's consumer guidance, for example, notes that a quote can be legally binding if it forms part of a contract. If you want certainty, ask for a quote rather than an estimate, ask for it in writing, and keep a copy. If the operator says the price may change, ask what would cause that and how you will be told before extra work is done.

Insurance: ask for the certificate

Public liability insurance covers a business if someone is injured or their property is damaged because of the business's negligence. Business.gov.au explains that it is required for certain occupations in some states and territories, and that other businesses choose to carry it where the risk of a claim is high. In other words, you cannot assume every operator has it.

For exterior cleaning, where water, chemicals and equipment are used around windows, cars, gardens and neighbours' property, it is reasonable to ask:

  • Do you hold public liability insurance?
  • Can you send me a current certificate of currency?
  • Does the business name on the certificate match the name on the quote?

A legitimate business can usually send a certificate of currency by email. If the answer is vague, treat that as information.

It is also worth checking your own home and contents policy so you understand what it does and doesn't cover if something goes wrong during work by a contractor. Policies vary, so ask your insurer rather than relying on general rules.

Check who you are dealing with

A few basic checks take minutes:

  • ABN. Ask for the business's ABN and look it up on the Australian Business Register's ABN Lookup to confirm the name and that it is active.
  • A street address. Queensland's Office of Fair Trading advises getting a quote and receipt that include the trader's name and street address, not only a first name and mobile number.
  • Licensing, where relevant. Whether a licence is needed depends on whether the work counts as building work. The QBCC requires a licence for building work valued over $3,300 (including labour, materials and GST), and it issues licences for trades such as painting and decorating. If a quote moves beyond cleaning into repairs, painting or coating, ask what licence applies and check it on the QBCC licence register.

Ask how run-off will be handled

In Queensland, waste water from outdoor cleaning is listed as a prescribed water contaminant under Schedule 10 of the Environmental Protection Regulation 2019. Under section 440ZG of the Environmental Protection Act 1994, it is an offence to deposit a prescribed water contaminant into a roadside gutter or stormwater drain, or where it could reasonably wash into one. Councils enforce this locally, and Redland City Council specifically lists steam and high-pressure cleaning among the businesses that must keep these contaminants out of the stormwater system.

So it is fair to ask:

  • Where will the water go?
  • Will any of it reach the street gutter or a stormwater pit, and how will you stop that?
  • What cleaning products will you use, and can I see the product names?
  • How will you protect my garden, pond or pool?

Council guidance generally allows water carrying only soil and organic matter to be directed onto lawn or garden, while water containing oil or chemicals needs to be collected and disposed of properly. A careful operator will have a clear, specific answer. Our article on wastewater and run-off explains the rules in more detail.

Ask how they will work at height

Falls from ladders and roofs are a serious, well-documented risk. WorkSafe Queensland publishes a code of practice on managing the risk of falls at workplaces, and in 2026 issued an incident alert after a worker died falling from a ladder while accessing a residential roof. WorkSafe treats ladders as a lower-order control, to be used only after safer options have been considered.

You don't need to assess an operator's safety system, but you can ask how they will reach second-storey walls, eaves or gutters. Extension poles and work from the ground are common ways to avoid ladders for some tasks. If ladders or roof access are needed, ask how they will be secured.

Ask too about overhead power lines. WorkSafe Queensland's guidance on exclusion zones notes that an electric shock can occur without touching a line, because being close to it can cause a flashover. Long metal poles and ladders near the service line to your house deserve care.

Door-to-door offers: know your rights

Some exterior cleaning, roofing and driveway work is sold by knocking on doors, sometimes with an offer to "do it now while we're in the street". Under the Australian Consumer Law, a sale that comes from an uninvited approach and is worth $100 or more is an unsolicited consumer agreement, and specific rules apply:

  • Hours. Door-to-door sellers may only call between 9 am and 6 pm on weekdays and 9 am and 5 pm on Saturdays, and not on Sundays or public holidays.
  • Leaving. You can ask a seller to leave at any time and they must go. The Federal Court has confirmed that a "Do Not Knock" sign counts as a request to leave. Queensland residents can order a free Do Not Knock sticker from the Office of Fair Trading.
  • Cooling-off. You have 10 business days to cancel for any reason. During that time, the trader generally cannot take payment or start work, and that includes deposits for materials.

The ACCC has reviewed these rules and recommended changes, so check its website for the current position if you are dealing with an uninvited offer.

Queensland's Office of Fair Trading has repeatedly warned about travelling traders, particularly after storms, who offer cheap on-the-spot work and then raise the price once work starts or take a deposit and don't return. In one case it reported, a couple quoted a maximum of $2,800 for a driveway was later billed $6,000. Its advice is not to pay anything up front, to get the trader's full name and street address, and to note the vehicle registration. If a trader refuses to leave or demands more money, call the police. To report a trader, contact the Office of Fair Trading on 13 QGOV (13 74 68).

Red flags worth taking seriously

None of these proves someone is dishonest, but each is worth a second thought:

  • A price given on the doorstep with pressure to decide immediately.
  • A request for cash or a large deposit before any work is done.
  • Only a first name and mobile number, with no business name, ABN or address.
  • No written quote, or a quote that doesn't name surfaces, methods or exclusions.
  • No clear answer about insurance or where the run-off will go.
  • One method for everything, such as high pressure on render, painted surfaces and timber alike.
  • Claims that sound absolute, such as "every stain will come out".
