Driveway stains explained: what's on your concrete

Not every mark on a driveway is dirt, and not every mark will come out. Knowing what a stain is, and what kind of concrete it sits on, tells you what cleaning can realistically achieve.

Key takeaways
  • Black and green patches are usually organic growth such as algae, moss or lichen, which thrive in damp, shaded spots and return over time.
  • Oil and rust soak into porous concrete. Fresh marks often lift; old ones usually lighten rather than disappear.
  • Sealing can help with staining and loose stones, but it adds a maintenance cycle and can make a surface more slippery.

Concrete looks solid, but it is porous. Water, oil and organic matter work their way into the surface, which is why a driveway darkens gradually over a few years and why some marks are much harder to shift than others.

What the black and green staining actually is

Most of the dark patches on Queensland driveways are living things rather than dirt. The UK's Royal Horticultural Society describes algae, lichens, liverworts and moss on hard surfaces as being caused by "damp, humid, shady conditions" and poor drainage. That matches what most people see: the worst growth is under trees, along the shaded side of the house and wherever water lingers.

There are a few practical points to know:

  • It is mostly cosmetic, but it is slippery. The RHS notes that these growths don't damage the surfaces they grow on, but they do make paths and steps slippery.
  • Lichen is the stubborn one. Consumer guides generally rank lichen as harder to shift than algae or mould, and the round, crusty spots often take more than one treatment.
  • It comes back. Cleaning removes what's there, but the conditions that grew it remain. Discussing lichen on cladding, the NZ Metal Roofing code of practice warns that "recolonisation is very likely", particularly in sheltered and shady areas. Trimming overhanging branches and fixing pooling water help more than anything else.

Oil, rust and tyre marks

Oil

Oil is difficult because it soaks in. Retail and trade guides agree that speed matters: the best result comes from treating a spill before it penetrates. For older stains, a poultice (an absorbent paste left on the stain to draw oil back out) is the usual approach, sometimes over several rounds. Deep, old oil stains on porous concrete usually lighten rather than vanish completely.

Rust

Orange-brown stains often come from metal objects left on the concrete, or from fertiliser. Lawn products containing iron sulphate can spill onto drives and leave rust-coloured marks. A US cooperative extension answer citing the Portland Cement Association describes an oxalic acid solution left on the stain for two to three hours, then scrubbed and rinsed. Oxalic acid is toxic and corrosive, so it needs gloves and eye protection, a test patch and a thorough rinse. Acid left on concrete keeps etching it.

Tyre marks

Black tyre marks on plain, unsealed concrete are usually surface rubber and grime. On sealed concrete, marks can be part of a different problem called "hot tyre pickup". Concrete Decor explains that tyres warm up while driving and contract as they cool. Where the sealer isn't well bonded, that contraction can pull it off in the pattern of the tread. The sealer isn't melting. It is an adhesion problem, so cleaning alone won't fix it.

Efflorescence: the white bloom that isn't dirt

White, powdery deposits on concrete or pavers are usually efflorescence: salts carried to the surface by moisture and left behind as the water evaporates. New Zealand's building research body, BRANZ, notes that efflorescence does not affect the performance of concrete, brickwork or blockwork, though it is unsightly.

Washing can make it seem to disappear, then return as the surface dries, because the salt has simply re-dissolved. Think Brick Australia's cleaning manual recommends brushing efflorescence off with a stiff, dry brush once the surface has dried out.

Plain, exposed aggregate and coated concrete are different jobs

Plain and broom-finished concrete

This is the most forgiving surface. Sound plain concrete handles a rotary surface cleaner well, provided the pressure suits the slab. Too much pressure held too close can etch the surface and leave marks. Older or poorly finished slabs need a gentler pass.

Exposed aggregate

Here the decorative stones sit in a thin cement paste. Aggressive cleaning can wear that paste away, and loose stones can come out. Concrete Decor notes that penetrating sealers won't secure loose stones. A film-forming acrylic sealer helps hold them in place, and stones that are already loose may need a bonding product. If your aggregate is already shedding stones, it's better to know before it is cleaned.

Stencilled, coloured and spray-on coated concrete

These driveways have a coating on top: a coloured layer, a stencil pattern and usually a sealer. Cleaning these is really about cleaning, and preserving, the coating. Where the coating has already degraded, cleaning tends to reveal the damage rather than cause it, and patches of old sealer can leave an uneven finish. In a Bunnings Workshop discussion, a member of the Bunnings team noted they knew of no coating manufacturer that would recommend or warrant a new coat over a degraded one. Assess these surfaces carefully before any pressure goes near them.

Should you seal after cleaning?

Sealing is a choice, not a requirement. There are good reasons on both sides.

Possible benefits

  • Stain resistance. Penetrating sealers create a water-repellent barrier within the concrete's pores, which slows how quickly water and spills soak in.
  • Holding aggregate. On exposed aggregate, a film-forming acrylic sealer helps keep stones in place.
  • Colour. Tinted, colour-enhancing sealers are sold for refreshing coloured and stencilled driveways.

Possible drawbacks

  • A maintenance cycle. Acrylic sealers wear and need reapplying. Concrete Decor puts acrylic-sealed exposed aggregate at a resealing interval of roughly 12 to 36 months, depending on weather and traffic. Penetrating sealers generally last longer. One Concrete Decor columnist argues that rigid "reseal every year or two" schedules cause many sealer failures, and that resealing should follow condition.
  • Slip resistance. A trade article in For Construction Pros notes that topical sealers can be slipperier, and that many manufacturers suggest penetrating sealers for outdoor areas that get wet.
  • Tyre pickup and peeling. Poorly bonded film sealers can lift under tyres or peel, and a failing sealer is harder to clean evenly than bare concrete.
  • Timing. Sealers are applied to a clean, dry surface. Stone supplier Sandstone World, for example, advises never sealing wet stone and keeping it dry for 24 hours afterwards. Sealing straight after a wash, before the slab has dried, works against you.

If you are considering sealing, choose the sealer for the surface. Penetrating sealers suit plain concrete where slip resistance matters. Film-forming sealers suit exposed aggregate and coloured finishes where holding stones or enhancing colour is the goal. Ask about the reapplication interval before you commit.

A word on runoff

What comes off a driveway has to go somewhere. Brisbane City Council lists driveway cleaning runoff among the water pollution it investigates, with on-the-spot fines possible under the Environmental Protection Act 1994. Redland City Council includes wastewater from outdoor cleaning among the contaminants residents must take steps to keep out of drains and gutters. Sweeping debris first, and directing wash water onto lawn or garden where practical, are sensible habits.

Setting realistic expectations

Cleaning removes what sits on a surface. It doesn't change the surface itself. Growth, grime and fresh spills usually respond well. Old oil, rust bleed, efflorescence and sun-faded colour tend to lighten rather than disappear, and etching or flaking is physical damage that needs repair rather than cleaning. A good contractor will tell you which category each mark falls into before starting. At Stride, that conversation happens at the assessment, so the result on the day isn't a surprise.

How often a driveway needs cleaning varies. Shade, tree cover and drainage matter far more than the calendar.
