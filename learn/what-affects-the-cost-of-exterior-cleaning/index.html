What affects the cost of exterior cleaning

Two homes on the same street can attract very different quotes for what sounds like the same job. Understanding what actually drives the time, equipment and risk involved makes it much easier to compare quotes fairly.

Key takeaways
  • Surface area and access usually matter more than the type of house, because they set how long the job takes.
  • Height adds cost because it changes the equipment and safety controls needed, not just the effort.
  • Containing and disposing of run-off properly is part of the job in Queensland, so a quote that ignores it is not comparable with one that includes it.

There is no single price for "cleaning the outside of a house", and anyone who gives you one without seeing the property is guessing. What you pay reflects how long the job will take, what equipment and products it needs, how much risk is involved, and what has to happen to the water afterwards.

This article explains the main price drivers so you can read a quote with a clearer eye. We have not included typical dollar figures: we could not find multiple credible, current, independent sources for Brisbane or Redlands pricing, and published "average" figures online tend to come from businesses selling the service.

1. Surface area

The biggest single factor is how much surface there is to clean. That sounds obvious, but area is easy to underestimate.

A "driveway" might be a single strip or a wide double slab with a turning bay. Add front and side paths, an alfresco area, a pool surround and a garage apron and the total hard surface can be several times what you first pictured. Walls work the same way: a single-storey brick home with deep eaves has a very different wall and soffit area from a two-storey rendered home.

When comparing quotes, check that each one lists the same surfaces. A cheaper quote may simply cover less.

2. Access

Access affects how quickly equipment can be set up and moved, and how much hose has to be run. Things that tend to add time include:

  • narrow side passages or steps between levels
  • gates, fences and garden beds that equipment has to work around
  • no vehicle access close to the area being cleaned
  • outdoor furniture, pot plants or stored items that need moving
  • distance from the nearest outside tap

Clearing furniture and pot plants yourself before the job can sometimes save time, but ask first: some operators prefer to move things themselves so they know where everything is.

3. Height and number of storeys

Height changes the job more than people expect. On a single-storey home, most walls, eaves and gutters can often be reached from the ground with poles. On a two-storey home, upper eaves, walls and gutters may need ladders, platforms or roof access.

WorkSafe Queensland's code of practice on managing the risk of falls requires businesses to manage fall risks, and its guidance treats ladders as a lower-order control that should be used only after safer options have been considered. In 2026 it issued an alert after a worker died falling from a ladder while accessing a residential roof. Safer ways of working at height usually take more equipment, more set-up time, and sometimes a second person, which is reflected in price.

Overhead power lines can also complicate access. WorkSafe Queensland sets exclusion zones around live lines, and notes that a shock can happen without direct contact. Where the service line to a house crosses the work area, the operator may need to change methods or equipment.

4. How dirty the surfaces are

Light dust and a thin film of growth come off much more quickly than years of build-up. Heavier soiling usually means longer dwell times for cleaning solutions, more passes, or a pre-treatment step.

Local conditions play a part. In the Redlands, Bureau of Meteorology records for the Redlands station show average annual rainfall of roughly 1,270 mm, with January to March the wettest months at around 160 mm each. Shaded, damp surfaces in that climate grow mould, moss and algae faster than sunny ones. Close to the bay, airborne salt adds another layer: corrosion guidance notes that salt deposits are heaviest near the shoreline and that sheltered surfaces such as the undersides of eaves aren't washed clean by rain, so build-up can be greater there.

Some marks are stains rather than dirt. Rust, oil, tannin from leaves, fertiliser and efflorescence (white salt deposits on masonry) may need specific treatments and may not fully disappear. A good quote says so in advance.

5. Surface type

Different materials need different methods, and slower methods cost more time:

  • Sound concrete and most pavers generally tolerate higher pressure and rotary surface cleaners, which cover ground quickly.
  • Render, painted surfaces, fibre cement and timber are usually cleaned at low pressure with a cleaning solution that is left to work and then rinsed. This is gentler but involves more steps.
  • Natural stone varies widely, and some stones are sensitive to acidic products or high pressure.

If a quote proposes one method for every surface, ask why.

6. Run-off control and disposal

In Queensland, waste water from outdoor cleaning is a prescribed water contaminant under Schedule 10 of the Environmental Protection Regulation 2019, and it is an offence under section 440ZG of the Environmental Protection Act 1994 to let it enter a roadside gutter or stormwater drain. Councils such as Redland City Council specifically name high-pressure cleaning businesses among those that must keep these contaminants out of stormwater.

Meeting that obligation takes time and equipment. Depending on the site, it can involve directing water onto lawn or garden (which council guidance generally allows only for water carrying soil and organic matter), blocking or protecting nearby drains, or collecting water for proper disposal. Discharging collected waste water to sewer needs approval from the local water authority; in the Redlands, that means trade waste approval from Redland City Council.

A property where the driveway falls straight to the street gutter, or a pool sits next to the area being cleaned, may need more containment work than a flat block surrounded by lawn. A quote that ignores run-off is not comparable to one that includes it.

7. Sealing and other add-ons

Some quotes include applying a sealer to concrete or pavers after cleaning. Sealing involves extra product, drying time and often a second visit, and the choice of sealer depends on the surface. Treat it as a separate line item so you can compare the cleaning price on its own.

If a quote includes painting or coating, ask what licence applies. The QBCC requires a licence for building work valued over $3,300, and painting and decorating is a licensed trade class.

8. Travel and scheduling

Travel time is a real cost for any mobile business. Properties outside an operator's usual area, or on islands reached by vehicle ferry, may attract a travel component or need to be scheduled alongside other nearby work. Some operators adjust prices for smaller jobs because set-up and pack-down take a similar amount of time regardless of job size.

9. Water supply

Most operators use mains water from an outside tap. Low tap pressure or flow can slow some equipment. In South East Queensland, Seqwater's water security program sets out when region-wide restrictions would apply (when combined dam levels fall to 50%). Restrictions can affect outdoor cleaning, so check the current status with your water retailer if you are booking during a dry spell.

How to compare quotes fairly

When you have more than one quote, line them up against the same questions:

  1. Do they cover the same surfaces?
  2. Does each surface have a stated method?
  3. Is run-off control included, and how?
  4. Are exclusions and likely limitations (stains that may not lift) stated?
  5. Is sealing or any other extra priced separately?
  6. Is the price fixed, and does it include GST?

If the answers line up, comparing price is straightforward. If they don't, the cheapest quote may simply describe a smaller or less careful job.
