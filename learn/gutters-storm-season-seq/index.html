Gutters and storm season in South-East Queensland

Gutters do nothing for most of the year, then have to move a summer storm's worth of water in minutes. Clearing them before storm season is one of the simplest things Queensland's own disaster-preparation advice asks homeowners to do.

Key takeaways
  • Aim to have gutters, valleys and downpipes clear before the severe-weather season, which the Bureau of Meteorology puts at October to April.
  • Blocked gutters can push water into eaves and roof cavities and dump it beside the house, where it can affect footings.
  • Insurers commonly distinguish sudden storm damage from damage caused by poor maintenance, so keep records of gutter cleaning.

Why timing matters in SEQ

South-East Queensland's rainfall is strongly seasonal. At the Bureau of Meteorology's Brisbane station (040913), mean monthly rainfall drops to around 29–34 mm in July, August and September, then climbs to 87 mm in October, 102 mm in November, 138 mm in December and a peak of 176 mm in February.

That pattern matters for gutters. Through the drier late-winter and spring months, leaves, seed pods, bark and roof grit build up quietly. Then the season turns. The Bureau describes October to April as Australia's peak period for severe weather, and notes that the highest thunderstorm risk on the east coast is in northern New South Wales and southern Queensland from October to December. The first big storm of the season often arrives when gutters are at their fullest.

Queensland Government disaster guidance is direct about timing: the best time to prepare your home is before storm, cyclone and monsoon season.

What happens when gutters overflow

A gutter that cannot drain does not simply stop working. The water goes somewhere else:

  • Into the eaves and roof cavity. Get Ready Queensland's home-maintenance guidance asks homeowners to keep gutters and downpipes clear of leaves and other blockages precisely so water doesn't pool in the eaves or roof cavity.
  • Onto fascias and soffits. Water spilling over the back of a gutter runs down the fascia board and into the eave lining. Over repeated storms, timber fascias and lining boards can stain, swell or rot.
  • Beside the house. Overflow that dumps along one wall concentrates water against the slab or footings. The Queensland Building and Construction Commission (QBCC) explains that, depending on soil type, footings move as the moisture content of the soil changes, and advises keeping discharged water away from the ground near footings.
  • Into the roof itself. The QBCC notes that roof leaks are often caused by a lack of maintenance and cleaning. Valleys, where two roof slopes meet, collect leaves and are a common overflow point.

How often should gutters be cleaned?

There is no single correct interval. It depends mostly on trees.

SituationReasonable starting point
Open block, few or no overhanging treesOnce a year, before storm season
Some nearby trees, or a neighbour's trees overhangingTwice a year: late winter/spring, and again after autumn leaf drop
Under gums, palms or heavy canopyCheck every few months and after big wind events
Rainwater tank connectedAs above, plus check first-flush devices and screens

Monier, a major Australian roof tile manufacturer, recommends cleaning gutters, downpipes and valleys at least once a year, and more often if trees surround the roof. If you only do it once, late winter to early spring (August to September) is the sensible window in SEQ: the dry months are when debris builds up, and the storm season is still ahead.

After a severe storm, it is worth a look from the ground. Hail, wind and fallen branches can fill a gutter in a single afternoon.

Don't forget the downpipes

A clean gutter that drains into a blocked downpipe still overflows. Signs of a downpipe problem include:

  • water sheeting over the gutter at one spot during heavy rain, usually near the outlet
  • a gurgling or bubbling sound at the top of the downpipe
  • a dry stormwater outlet in the yard when it's pouring
  • puddling or erosion where the downpipe meets the ground.

Leaf build-up at the outlet (the "pop" where the gutter joins the downpipe) is the most common culprit. Problems deeper in the stormwater pipes underground are a job for a licensed plumber.

Gutter guards: helpful, not maintenance-free

Gutter mesh and guards reduce how much leaf litter enters the gutter, and mesh manufacturers advise choosing a sturdy product with an aperture suited to the debris your trees drop. They are a good investment under heavy tree cover. But they change the maintenance rather than ending it:

  • Fine material such as grit, pollen and small seeds can still pass through or collect on top of mesh.
  • Leaves can mat on top of guards and shed water over the edge in a downpour.
  • Valleys, box gutters and outlets still need checking.

Treat guards as a way to stretch the interval between cleans, not to skip them, and ask whoever installs them how the mesh is meant to be cleared.

Storm-season preparation checklist

Get Ready Queensland, the Queensland Government's disaster-preparedness program, publishes home-maintenance checklists. The roof and gutter items, in summary:

  • Keep gutters and downpipes clear of leaves and other blockages.
  • Check the roof's condition. Repair loose or cracked tiles, loose screws and damaged flashing, eaves or guttering.
  • Check for tree branches hanging over the roof and cut them back if you can do so safely.
  • Secure or store loose outdoor items such as furniture and trampolines.
  • Use a licensed tradesperson for repairs. Get Ready Queensland notes that damaged tiles, iron, gutters or eaves may need a roofer.

The Queensland SES storm page and Get Ready Queensland's printable dangerous-weather checklist (both linked below) are worth keeping on the fridge.

Insurance: keep a record

Home insurance generally covers sudden events like storms, not damage caused by gradual deterioration or lack of maintenance. A 2023 review by the General Insurance Code Governance Committee found that 55 per cent of the 42,956 denied home insurance claims it examined relied on wear-and-tear or maintenance exclusions. In one dispute reported by Insurance News, the Australian Financial Complaints Authority accepted an insurer's position that water entering a property through gutters congested with leaves was not an insured event.

Every policy is different, and the Code Governance Committee itself noted that what counts as "acceptable maintenance" is often poorly explained. The Insurance Council of Australia's practical advice is simple: clear gutters, deal with roof damage, and keep records of the work. A dated photo of clean gutters before storm season costs nothing and could help if you ever need to claim.

Doing it yourself, safely

Single-storey gutters with good ground access are a reasonable DIY job for someone confident on a ladder. But ladders are the main injury risk. The Queensland Government reports that more than 1,000 Queenslanders are hospitalised each year after falling off ladders, most of them around the home. If you do it yourself:

  • Use a ladder that meets AS/NZS 1892, on firm level ground, and have someone with you.
  • Don't lean out sideways. Move the ladder instead.
  • Stay off the roof, and never work on it when it's wet.
  • Leave two-storey, steep or tile-edge sections to someone with the right access equipment.

Many homeowners choose to have gutters cleared professionally at least once a year, often alongside a roof or solar clean (it's a service Stride offers across the Redlands and bayside Brisbane). Whoever does it, the goal is the same: water off the roof, into the downpipes, and away from the house before the first big storm arrives.
