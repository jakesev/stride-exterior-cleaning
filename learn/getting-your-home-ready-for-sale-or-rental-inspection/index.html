Getting your home's exterior ready for sale or inspection

The outside of a home is the first thing buyers see online and the first thing an inspector walks past. A little planning about what to clean, and when, makes a visible difference without turning into a renovation.

Key takeaways
  • Focus on the surfaces that show in photos and on the way to the front door: driveway, entry path, front wall, eaves and the alfresco area.
  • Book cleaning close enough to photos that growth hasn't returned, but with time to deal with anything the clean reveals.
  • In a Queensland tenancy, the tenant must leave the premises as far as possible in the condition they were in at the start, fair wear and tear excepted. The entry condition report is the benchmark.

Whether you are selling, getting ready for a routine inspection, or handing back a rental, the exterior is where first impressions are formed. It is also where problems show most clearly in photographs: a dark driveway, streaked render or green paths can make a well-kept home look neglected.

This guide covers what to prioritise, how to time the work, and what Queensland tenancy law says about the outside of a rental property.

Start with what people actually see

Walk out to the street and look back at the house, then take a photo on your phone. What stands out in the photo is usually what will stand out in the listing.

For most homes, the priorities are:

  1. Driveway and front path. These take up a large part of the front photo and are the route buyers walk. Even, consistent concrete reads as "looked after"; patchy or stained concrete draws the eye.
  2. The front wall and entry. Streaks under gutters and window sills, dirty eaves and cobwebs around the front door are noticeable up close.
  3. The alfresco or entertaining area. It often features in listing photos, and slippery or stained paving is the first thing people notice when they step outside.
  4. Pool surrounds, if you have a pool. Coping and paving around a pool are visible in photos and walked on barefoot.
  5. Gutters and fascia. Overflowing gutters or plants growing from them are visible from the street and can raise questions about maintenance.
  6. Windows and screens, particularly those facing the street or the view.

Side paths, bin areas and the back fence matter less for photos but do matter for inspections, especially for slip hazards and drainage.

Clean first, then decide on repairs

Cleaning sometimes reveals problems that dirt was hiding: paint that is chalky or lifting, cracked render, loose pavers, rust marks or worn sealer. That is useful information, but it is better to find it two or three weeks before photos than the day before.

If cleaning shows up paint failure, you may decide to touch up the most visible areas. If you are engaging someone for painting or repairs, the QBCC requires a licence for building work valued over $3,300, and painting and decorating is a licensed trade class. Check the licence on the QBCC register.

Timing around the weather

The Redlands and Brisbane have a summer-wet climate. Bureau of Meteorology records for the Redlands station show average annual rainfall of roughly 1,270 mm, with January to March the wettest months (around 160 mm each) and late winter to early spring the driest (around 52 mm in August and 38 mm in September).

That has two practical effects:

  • Growth returns faster in the wet months. Shaded surfaces can start to darken again within weeks during a wet summer. If your photos are in summer, book cleaning closer to the shoot.
  • Rain can push back cleaning and photos. Leave a buffer in your schedule. Most exterior cleaning is rescheduled in heavy rain or storms.

As a general guide, many sellers aim to have exterior cleaning done in the week or two before photography, then a quick sweep and leaf-blow on the morning of the shoot. If gutters are full, cleaning them before the walls avoids dirty overflow streaking freshly cleaned surfaces in the next rain.

Bayside homes have an extra consideration. Corrosion guidance notes that airborne salt is deposited most heavily close to the shoreline and that sheltered surfaces, such as the undersides of eaves, aren't rinsed by rain. A salt film can make windows and painted surfaces look dull, and it builds up again over time, so timing the clean close to the listing helps.

If you are a tenant: what the law says about the exterior

In Queensland, residential tenancies are governed by the Residential Tenancies and Rooming Accommodation Act 2008, and the Residential Tenancies Authority (RTA) administers bonds and publishes guidance.

Two obligations under the Act matter most here:

  • During the tenancy, the tenant must keep the premises clean, having regard to their condition at the start of the tenancy.
  • At the end of the tenancy, the tenant must leave the premises, as far as possible, in the same condition they were in at the start, fair wear and tear excepted.

The property manager or owner, for their part, must keep the premises fit to live in and in good repair.

The entry condition report is the benchmark

At the start of a tenancy, the condition of the property is recorded in an entry condition report, and at the end it is compared against an exit condition report. Those records, including any photos, are what matters if there is a disagreement about the bond.

That means:

  • If the driveway was already stained or the paths were already green at move-in and the entry report records that, a tenant is generally not expected to return them in better condition than they received them.
  • If the entry report records clean paving and the outdoor areas are now heavily soiled through use, that may be raised at the exit inspection.
  • Gradual weathering and normal wear are not the tenant's responsibility. Damage, spills (such as oil from a vehicle) or neglect may be treated differently.

Read your tenancy agreement too. It may include special terms about lawns, gardens, gutters or pools, and those terms set out who is responsible for what. Some tasks, such as cleaning high gutters or working on roofs, may involve working at height, and you can raise with the property manager whether they are the owner's responsibility.

If there is a dispute about the bond or the condition of the property, the RTA offers a free dispute resolution service.

Practical tips for tenants

  • Find your entry condition report and photos before you start, so you know the standard you are working to.
  • Sweep paths and the alfresco area, clear cobwebs and remove any marks you caused (for example, oil drips on the driveway).
  • If you hire a cleaner, keep the invoice and take dated photos after the work.
  • Avoid DIY high-pressure cleaning on render, painted surfaces or timber unless you know what you are doing, because damage can create a bigger problem than the original dirt.

Safety and run-off if you do it yourself

Two points apply to anyone, owner or tenant:

  • Ladders and roofs are a common cause of serious injury. WorkSafe Queensland treats ladders as a lower-order control, to be used only after safer options have been considered, and the national product safety regulator reports thousands of ladder-fall hospitalisations each year. If you need to reach second-storey eaves or gutters, consider whether it is worth the risk.
  • Keep cleaning water out of the street gutter. In Queensland, waste water from outdoor cleaning is a prescribed water contaminant, and it is an offence to let it reach a roadside gutter or stormwater drain. Direct it onto lawn or garden where possible, and don't rinse chemicals or oil into drains.

A pre-listing or pre-inspection checklist

  • Driveway and front path cleaned evenly
  • Front wall, entry and eaves free of streaks and cobwebs
  • Gutters clear and not overflowing
  • Alfresco and pool surrounds clean and not slippery
  • Street-facing windows clean
  • Bins, hoses and outdoor clutter tidied out of sight
  • Any paint or render issues revealed by cleaning noted and, if needed, fixed
  • For tenants: entry condition report checked and after-photos taken
